## 2. Problem — the house always wins, and it sells you hope to make sure of it

**Headline**
> **IN EVERY GAME OF CHANCE, SOMEONE ALREADY DECIDED WHO WINS.**

**Body, two columns**

| Every other game of chance | Tywish |
|---|---|
| Someone with money and lawyers wrote the winning condition once, in fine print, and locked it. | The winning condition is written and elected in public, before every draw. |
| You pick numbers. You wait. You hope. | You pick numbers. You can also write how winning works. |
| The house's edge was never the math — it's that only the house got to write the rule. | Anyone can write a rule. The community decides which one plays. |

**Closing line**
> **The house never needed to load the dice. It just made sure it was the only one holding the pen.**

*Why this over the original draft's version:* the original ("they choose the rules, you wait, you hope") described the mechanic without naming what's exploitative about it. This version names the exploit — hope sold by whoever already holds the pen — which is the emotional core the brief asked for, without claiming the draw itself can be controlled (that claim would be false and shouldn't be made).

## 4. Concept — luck is spread on purpose, so no one can buy the whole round

**Headline**
> **A DEEP POCKET CAN'T WRITE EVERY RULE. LUCK STILL DECIDES BETWEEN THEM.**

**Body**
> Every round, several rules compete for the community's backing. Which one becomes official is decided by votes, not by whoever spent the most. And which sequence wins under that rule is still decided by a public, verifiable draw — money can buy a proposal, it can't buy the outcome.
>
> That's the actual defense against the thing every other game of chance is vulnerable to: **a small number of large players quietly deciding the terms for everyone else.** Here the terms are public, contested every round, and settled by chance at the end — not by whoever showed up with the most capital.

**Cards**

**FOUR IS ENOUGH** — Match at least four drawn numbers.
**SAME TOTAL** — Your six numbers add up to the same total as the draw.
**UNDERDOG** — Every selected number is lower than its drawn counterpart.
**YOUR RULE** — Write another way to win.

**Closing line**
> **Every round can become a different game — and no one gets to pick which one alone.**

*Why this section changed:* the brief specifically asked for "luck spread across events so a few resourceful actors can't concentrate/manipulate power" — this was entirely absent from the original draft, which only used randomness to prove the draw wasn't rigged (a different claim). This version states the anti-concentration argument directly, but scopes it correctly: it's about who gets to **author and elect the rule**, not about equal odds of winning a random draw (an equal-odds claim would be false and shouldn't be made — see the note at the end of this document).

## 7. JDR — one screen, not three

Consolidates what the original draft spread across three sections (introducing JDR, explaining the fee pool, and drawing the full economic loop). Keep the mechanic explanation to this single section; link out for anyone who wants the full mechanics.

**Headline**
> **THE MONEY THE GAME MAKES GOES BACK TO THE PEOPLE PLAYING IT.**

**Body**
> JDR is the token that connects participation to the Tywish economy. It has a fixed supply, it's earned through eligible game activity, and it can be locked to share in the fees the game itself generates.

**Three numbers**

**1 BILLION** — maximum JDR supply
**ZERO** — new JDR that can ever be minted after genesis
**200 MILLION** — set aside at genesis to pay players during the campaign

**How locking works, in one line**
> Lock JDR → the game generates fees from play → your share of the fee pool depends on how much you lock and for how long.

**The line that matters most**
> **PLAYERS CREATE THE ECONOMY. PLAYERS RECEIVE THE ECONOMY.**

`See full tokenomics →`

*Why this consolidation:* the brief asked to treat tokenomics "superficially." Three consecutive sections re-explaining the same lock → weight → share mechanic from three angles (JDR intro, fee pool steps, economic-loop diagram) is the opposite of superficial — it's the heaviest stretch of the whole page, sitting right where a visitor has already decided whether to play. One section, three numbers, one link out, is enough.

**A consistency note worth keeping in mind when this ships:** the fee pool is weighted by *how much JDR you lock × how long* — that's a capital-and-time-weighted mechanic by design, and honestly so; it rewards commitment, not skill or luck. Don't let its copy sit close enough to Section 4's anti-concentration message that a careful reader could read them as contradicting each other (Section 4 is about who gets to write and elect a *rule*; Section 7 is a separate, openly capital-weighted reward for locking a token). Keeping them in separate sections, as here, avoids that collision.
